The House With No Address

— kenshi iwahashi —

Chapter One: The House at the End of the Road

There was a house at the end of Briar Lane that did not appear on any map. It stood beyond the last streetlight, hidden behind a wall of tangled vines and ancient trees, where the road narrowed into a forgotten path of cracked pavement. Its windows were tall and dark, its roof sagged beneath the weight of years, and its front gate hung open as though someone had left in a hurry and never returned. Despite its age, the house remained strangely untouched by the passing seasons. No matter how fiercely the rain fell or how violently the wind swept through the trees, its doors stayed firmly shut, and its windows never broke. The people of the nearby town called it the Addressless House, though no one could remember who had given it that name. It had no number on its gate, no name on its mailbox, and no record of ownership in the town's official documents. Delivery drivers claimed their navigation systems failed whenever they approached the lane. Surveyors found the property missing from land records, while old maps showed an empty stretch of woodland where the house now stood. Some residents believed it had been built illegally decades ago. Others whispered that the house had always been there, waiting for someone to notice it. The strangest part was not its absence from official records, but the stories surrounding those who had found it. Years earlier, a traveling salesman had reportedly entered the house during a storm to seek shelter. He emerged the following morning with no memory of the night before and a photograph of a family he had never met. A local schoolteacher once claimed that she had seen a light moving from room to room inside the abandoned building, even though the electricity had been disconnected for generations. Most disturbing of all were the accounts of people who insisted they had visited the house as children, only to discover that their memories no longer matched the rooms they remembered. Over time, the stories became little more than local folklore. Parents warned their children to stay away from Briar Lane, and teenagers dared one another to approach the gate after sunset. No one ever stayed long enough to uncover the truth. Those who entered usually returned with vague explanations, describing endless corridors, unfamiliar photographs, and doors that opened into rooms that seemed impossible to fit inside the building. Then, after years of silence, a letter arrived at the town's oldest post office. The envelope bore no stamp, no return address, and no name belonging to a known resident. The only writing on its surface was a street name that had been removed from every map in the region. Inside was a single sheet of paper containing a short message, written in careful black ink. The house remembers everyone who has entered. It is time someone remembered who built it. Beneath the message was a photograph of the Addressless House, taken from the front gate. The image appeared ordinary at first, showing the dark windows and overgrown garden beneath a cloudy sky. But in the second-floor window, a pale figure stood behind the glass, its face turned toward the camera. The postmaster recognized the figure. It was Eleanor Vale, a woman who had disappeared thirty years earlier and whose missing-person case had never been solved. By the following morning, the letter had vanished from the post office, and the postmaster could no longer recall who had delivered it. The photograph, however, remained on his desk. On its back, a new line of handwriting had appeared, though the paper showed no sign of having been altered. Find the room without a door. That same afternoon, a young woman named Amelia Hart returned to the town after receiving news that her grandmother had passed away. Among the belongings left behind was an old brass key tied to a faded ribbon. Amelia had never seen the key before, yet something about it felt strangely familiar. Its surface was engraved with a symbol she remembered from the margins of her childhood drawings: a small house surrounded by seven black circles. When she asked her relatives about it, no one could provide an explanation. Her grandmother had rarely spoken about her early life, and whenever Amelia mentioned the abandoned house on Briar Lane, the older woman had always changed the subject. Now, with the key resting in her palm, Amelia began to understand that her grandmother's silence had not been accidental. That evening, she followed the narrow road beyond the final streetlight. The trees grew thicker as she approached the property, their branches twisting overhead until the fading daylight disappeared. At the end of the path stood the house, exactly as it appeared in the photograph. The gate creaked when she touched it, though no wind stirred the leaves around her. Amelia climbed the steps and placed the brass key into the lock. It turned without resistance. Inside, the air smelled of dust, rain, and something faintly familiar, like the forgotten rooms of a childhood home. A staircase rose toward the darkness above, while a long corridor stretched into the distance, lined with portraits whose faces had been carefully scratched away. At the far end hung a mirror covered with a white cloth. Beneath it sat a small wooden table. On the table lay a guestbook, opened to a fresh page. Amelia approached it slowly. The earlier pages were filled with names, some written centuries apart, others dated only a few years before. Many had been crossed out, while a handful were marked with small black circles matching the symbol on her key. At the bottom of the final page, a name had already been written. Amelia Hart. Beside it was the date of her arrival. And beneath that, in handwriting she recognized as her grandmother's, were six words that made the silence of the house feel suddenly alive. You were never supposed to return. Somewhere upstairs, a door slammed shut. Then came the sound of footsteps moving toward the staircase. Amelia turned toward the entrance, but the front door had disappeared. The house had finally accepted its newest guest. And it had no intention of letting her leave.
