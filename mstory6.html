Clues Written in Fading Ink

— kenshi iwahashi —

Chapter One: The Letter That Should Not Exist

Some secrets were buried beneath layers of dust, hidden inside forgotten rooms, or carried silently by people who had spent their entire lives pretending to forget. Others were written down, carefully preserved in the hope that one day someone would discover the truth. In the quiet town of Ashbourne, where old houses stood behind iron gates and narrow streets disappeared beneath the morning fog, the past had a way of returning when no one expected it. For decades, the town had been known for its peaceful appearance and its grand historical library, a building that stood at the center of the oldest district. Its shelves held thousands of books, handwritten journals, and personal letters donated by families who had lived in Ashbourne for generations. Most of the documents were ordinary records of everyday life, but a small collection remained locked away in the library's restricted archive, untouched since the mysterious disappearance of a respected historian named Arthur Whitmore. Arthur had vanished twenty years earlier without leaving a note or explanation. He had been researching the history of several prominent families when he suddenly abandoned his work, leaving behind an unfinished manuscript and a desk covered in scattered papers. The authorities searched his home, questioned his colleagues, and examined every document in his possession, but they found no evidence that could explain his disappearance. Eventually, the investigation was closed, and his name became another forgotten entry in the town's history. Yet one question had remained unanswered: why had Arthur spent the final months of his life collecting letters written in different hands, all containing the same unusual phrase? The truth fades, but it never disappears. Years later, a young archivist named Clara Bennett inherited the responsibility of organizing the library's neglected records. She had always been fascinated by old documents, especially those that carried traces of lives long forgotten. To her, every faded signature and every stain on a page suggested a story waiting to be understood. She believed that the smallest details often revealed what official records failed to explain. While sorting through a collection of damaged books in the restricted archive, Clara discovered a narrow envelope pressed between the pages of an old history volume. Its paper had yellowed with age, and the edges crumbled slightly beneath her fingers. There was no name on the front, only a date written in faded blue ink—the same date Arthur Whitmore had disappeared. Inside lay a single sheet of paper covered in hurried handwriting. Most of the words had nearly vanished, leaving only fragments of sentences scattered across the page. Clara could make out references to a locked room, a missing photograph, and a person who had been seen at the library after closing time. At the bottom of the letter, beneath a dark ink stain, someone had written a name that appeared to have been deliberately scratched away. The handwriting belonged to Arthur Whitmore. Clara knew it immediately from the historian's surviving notes. But what unsettled her most was the message written across the reverse side of the paper, where the ink had faded so severely that the words were almost invisible. Only one sentence remained clear. Do not trust the person who finds this. The discovery should have been reported to the library director and added to the official archive. Instead, Clara noticed something unusual about the envelope. A faint series of numbers had been pressed into the paper, visible only when she held it beneath the desk lamp. The numbers formed a date, followed by a sequence that resembled the catalog reference of a book that did not appear in the library's records. By the following morning, that book had appeared on her desk. It was a small, leather-bound journal with no title, no author's name, and no entry in the library catalog. Its pages contained lists of dates, abbreviated names, and brief descriptions of events that had never appeared in local newspapers. Several entries referred to people who had died under mysterious circumstances, while others described disappearances officially recorded as accidents or voluntary departures. Near the middle of the journal, Clara found a page marked with a faded red line. Beneath it was a list of seven names, each accompanied by a date and a short handwritten note. The final name was Arthur Whitmore's. Beside it, someone had written a date from the following week. Clara checked the calendar, then returned to the journal, certain that she had misunderstood what she was seeing. The date was not a record of the past. It was a date that had not yet arrived. Beneath the list, nearly erased by time, was one final sentence. When the last clue disappears, the next name will be yours. Outside the archive, the library clock struck six. The building's lights flickered once, and somewhere among the towering shelves, a book fell to the floor. Clara turned toward the sound, but the aisle was empty. When she looked back at the journal, the ink beside the final name had begun to fade. And slowly, letter by letter, a new name was appearing beneath it. Her own.
